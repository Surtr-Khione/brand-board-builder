## The long version (founder letter, About page)

**I used to think branding was a tax.**

I came up as a direct response marketer. My world was the click, the conversion and the cost of both. If an ad
couldn't show me a number by Friday, it didn't deserve a budget on Monday. Brand people talked about feelings and
"awareness." I talked about return on ad spend. I thought we were in different businesses, and I thought mine was
the honest one. `[NEED: one real line or moment from those years, e.g. the campaign, the client, the thing you used
to say about brand work.]`

Then I joined a company that had the opposite problem from every client I'd ever had. `[NEED: the company, or "a
young company" if it stays unnamed; what it sold; when.]` It had a beautiful brand. People loved it on sight. They
shared it, remembered it, asked for it by name. What it didn't have was a single ad that could prove it made money.
No funnel, no tracking, no engine.

So I did what I knew. I built the engine. And the numbers came back wrong, in the good direction.
`[NEED: what happened, in real numbers you are willing to publish: cost per acquisition vs your old benchmarks,
conversion rate, growth over what period.]` The same playbook I'd run for years worked better than it ever had,
and it wasn't because I'd gotten smarter. People were already halfway sold when they saw the ad. They searched for
the name. They paid full price. The brand was doing work my dashboards had never given it credit for.

That's when the skeptic in me turned into a scientist.

**It turns out the research had been saying this for a decade.** Les Binet and Peter Field studied 996 advertising
campaigns from 700 brands in 83 categories in the IPA's effectiveness databank. Their conclusion reads like it was
written for the version of me that hated branding: "Short term effects are vital for efficiency. But long term
effects drive growth and profit. You need both." In their data, campaigns with more strong brand effects were far
more likely to report people becoming much less sensitive to price: 1% of campaigns with no big brand effects,
11% of campaigns with four or more. Brand is what lets the response work at a decent price.

One of the most famous launches of the last fifteen years ran exactly this play. In 2012, Dollar Shave Club spent $4,500 on a launch video
and signed up about 12,000 people in the first 48 hours. Then it did the unglamorous part: it bought TV only where its
attribution model showed the ad "delivered this many incremental orders." Its founder said the goal was "about 50%
paid and 50% organic." Four years later, Unilever bought it for a reported $1 billion, about five times annual sales.
(It wasn't a fairy tale after that. Unilever's own CEO later said the business "did not deliver as expected." A great
brand doesn't fix a hard business model. It does make a good one go faster.)

Airbnb showed the other side in its IPO filing. When it cut paid marketing in 2020, the traffic didn't vanish: about
91% of it already came "organically through direct or unpaid channels, reflecting the strength of our brand."

**So I opened the lab.** I started taking great brands apart the way I used to take apart ad accounts. What does
Apple's voice actually do that a competitor's doesn't? Which assets make Coca-Cola recognizable from across a store?
Why does one brand get a premium and its rival a discount? I pulled out the parts: the mission and the enemy, the
archetype, the voice rules, the distinctive assets, the palette, the proof. Then I did the thing a direct response
marketer can't help doing. I tried to score them.

That lab became BrandMD. The 85 brands in our library are the specimens. The Gravity Score is the measurement: a
number for how complete and defined a brand is, and a ranked list of what to fix first. The board is the full system
written down, so a team can actually use it. Brand Check and Drift Watch are the controls, so the brand you built is
the brand you ship.

**Why it matters to the price of a company.** Here's what I believe, from years on both sides of the table: I have
rarely seen a company with a forgettable brand sell for a big multiple. That's my opinion, not a study. But the
evidence points the same way. When companies are bought in consumer, food and retail, buyers book a median 11% of
the entire price to the trademarks and trade names alone, according to Houlihan Lokey's study of 2019 deals. A
meta-analysis of 83 studies found that strong marketing assets, brand among them, move a company's value far more
than ad spend does. And about 90% of the S&P 500's market value is now intangible, which includes the brand.

I still believe in the click. I just don't believe in the click alone anymore. BrandMD exists so you don't need ten years and a lucky job change to learn what I learned: **measure the brand like
you measure the ads, build it on purpose, and it will do the work your dashboard can't see.** Give your brand gravity.

## The short version (homepage, deck, About intro), about 110 words

I was a direct response marketer who thought branding was a tax. Then I joined a company with a beautiful brand and
no ad engine, built the engine, and watched it work better than anything I'd run before. The brand was doing the
work my dashboards couldn't see. The research had said it for years: Binet and Field's study of 996 campaigns found
brand and response need each other. So I started taking great brands apart and scoring them. That lab became
BrandMD: a score for your brand, the full system to fix it, and checks so it stays fixed. Measure the brand like you
measure the ads.

## The one-liner (bio, social)

A direct response marketer who used to think branding was a tax built the tool that measures it.

## Story notes

- **Point:** Brand and direct response multiply each other, and brand can be measured. BrandMD is the instrument.
- **Shape:** Story Circle (comfort: DR certainty → need: a brand-rich company with no engine → adapt: build the
  engine → find: the brand was doing the work → price: giving up the old belief → return: the lab → change: BrandMD).
  The Magician beat is the turn ("the numbers came back wrong, in the good direction").
- **Archetype fit:** the Sage tells the truth carefully (sources, the DSC caveat, opinion labeled as opinion); the
  Magician turns scattered facts into one mechanism (the lab, the score).
- **Claims we avoid on purpose:** "bad brands never sell for multiples" as fact (no study tests it, so it is stated as
  opinion); any "brand makes ads X% cheaper" number (none verified); "90% of value is brand" (Ocean Tomo measures all
  intangibles); Kantar's own portfolio returns (vendor data).
- **Other verified examples for decks** (in the evidence file): Beats ($3 billion to Apple, 2014); Liquid Death
  ($1.4 billion valuation, 2024); Red Bull; Glossier; Oatly; and the cautionary Allbirds (valued at $3.3 billion at
  IPO, assets sold for an estimated $39 million).
